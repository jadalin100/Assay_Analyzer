POST-HOC STATISTICAL ANALYSIS GUIDE
=====================================
Resazurin Smartphone Assay — N = 4 trials, 9 concentration groups
Jada Lin | 2026


OVERVIEW
--------
Five of six tested metrics showed a statistically significant overall
difference across concentration groups. Post-hoc tests identified which
specific concentration pairs drove those differences.


WHAT IS A POST-HOC TEST?
-------------------------
- The overall tests (ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis) only tell you "at least one
  group is different from the others."
- Post-hoc tests follow up by comparing every possible pair of groups
  to find out WHICH specific groups differ.
- With 9 groups, that means 36 pairwise comparisons per metric.


THE MULTIPLE COMPARISON PROBLEM
--------------------------------
- Every statistical test carries a 5% chance of a false positive
  (finding a difference that isn't really there).
- If you run 36 tests, you would expect roughly 1-2 false positives
  just by chance — even if nothing is truly different.
- A correction is needed to keep the total false positive rate at 5%
  across all 36 comparisons, not just each individual one.

  SIMPLE ANALOGY: If you roll a die once and get a six, that's notable.
  If you roll it 36 times and find at least one six, that's expected.
  The correction raises the bar so that only genuinely rare results pass.


TWO POST-HOC TESTS WERE USED — WHY DIFFERENT ONES?
----------------------------------------------------
This depends on which overall test was used for each metric.

  TUKEY HSD (used after one-way ANOVA)
  - Tukey was designed specifically for the problem of comparing all
    pairs simultaneously.
  - The multiple comparison correction is built directly into its formula
    using a special probability distribution (the studentized range
    distribution).
  - No separate correction needed — it's already accounted for.

  DUNN'S TEST + BONFERRONI (used after Kruskal-Wallis)
  - Dunn's test is essentially repeated pairwise comparisons on ranked
    data — conceptually similar to running repeated t-tests on ranks.
  - Unlike Tukey, Dunn's formula treats each comparison independently
    and doesn't know you ran 35 others.
  - Bonferroni correction is applied manually: each p-value is
    multiplied by the number of comparisons (36), raising the bar
    for significance.

  KEY DIFFERENCE IN ONE SENTENCE:
  Tukey has the correction built in. Dunn's needs it added on top.


ASSUMPTION TESTS RUN BEFORE CHOOSING WHICH TEST
-------------------------------------------------
Before choosing ANOVA vs. Kruskal-Wallis for each metric:

  Shapiro-Wilk test (normality)
  - Tests whether each group's data follows a normal distribution.
  - Used because N=4 per group is too small for most other normality tests.
  - If any group failed (p < 0.05) → Kruskal-Wallis was used instead of ANOVA.

  Levene's test (equal variances)
  - Tests whether all groups have similar spread/variance.
  - Used to confirm ANOVA's assumption of homogeneity of variance.
  - Median-based version used because it is robust to non-normality.


RESULTS BY METRIC
------------------

1. R/B AUC  [One-way ANOVA: F = 8.55, p < 0.001]
   Post-hoc: Tukey HSD
   Finding:
   - 10^8 (positive control) is significantly HIGHER than every other
     group (all 8 comparisons significant, p <= 0.001).
   - 10^7 is NOT significantly different from any group.
   - Takeaway: Only the highest bacterial load (10^8) drives a detectable
     R/B signal above all others.

2. Final R/B Ratio  [One-way ANOVA: F = 25.37, p < 0.001]
   Post-hoc: Tukey HSD
   Note: Levene's test failed (unequal variances across groups). Games-Howell
   would be more conservative, but given F = 25.37 the conclusion is robust.
   Finding:
   - 10^7 is significantly HIGHER than all groups except 10^8 (p <= 0.001).
   - 10^8 is significantly HIGHER than every single other group (p <= 0.001).
   - 10^6 is also significantly higher than sterile negative (p = 0.047).
   - Takeaway: At the final timepoint (t = 120 min), both 10^7 and 10^8
     show clear, significant separation. This is the strongest and cleanest
     result in the dataset.

3. Final G/B Ratio  [Kruskal-Wallis: H = 19.74, p = 0.011]
   Post-hoc: Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction
   Finding:
   - 10^6 is significantly LOWER than sterile negative (p = 0.036).
   - 10^6 is significantly LOWER than 10^8 (p = 0.036).
   - Takeaway: Unusual U-shaped pattern — G/B drops sharply at 10^6
     then partially recovers at 10^8. Reflects that G and B channels
     co-vary during the reaction, making this ratio less intuitive to
     interpret than R/B or R/G.

4. R/G AUC  [Kruskal-Wallis: H = 17.27, p = 0.027]
   Post-hoc: Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction
   Finding:
   - No specific pairwise pairs survived Bonferroni correction.
   - Takeaway: The overall test detected a real, diffuse effect across
     groups, but with N=4 there is insufficient power to pinpoint which
     specific pair is driving it. More trials would resolve this.
   - How to present: "Kruskal-Wallis revealed a significant overall group
     effect (p = 0.027), but pairwise comparisons did not identify specific
     concentration pairs after Bonferroni correction, likely due to limited
     statistical power at N = 4."

5. Final R/G Ratio  [Kruskal-Wallis: H = 18.91, p = 0.015]
   Post-hoc: Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction
   Finding:
   - No specific pairwise pairs survived Bonferroni correction.
   - Same interpretation as R/G AUC above.
   - How to present: Same sentence, substituting the relevant statistic.
